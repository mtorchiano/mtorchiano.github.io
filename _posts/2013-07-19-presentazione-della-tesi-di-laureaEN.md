At every graduation session, I find myself reviewing the slides for the theses I supervised before my students present them to the examination committee. Then I sit through the presentations.

Here are some principles and guidelines I usually give my students, both as a reminder to myself and in the hope that they may help others.

# BASIC PRINCIPLES

- **Presentation:** This is a presentation. Its purpose is to capture the audience’s attention and convey information that shows the value of the work you have done. Cut anything that does not serve these goals or that takes time and attention away from them.

- **Rules:** Presentations take place within a set of rules covering dress code, language, scheduling, and the time allowed. Know the rules and follow them. Breaking them may irritate the people listening to and evaluating you.

- **Time:** Time is precious and must be used wisely. Every moment wasted is information you cannot convey and time taken from your audience.

- **Data/ink:** Every pixel that is not part of the background should convey information. Edward Tufte’s [data-ink ratio](http://www.infovis-wiki.net/index.php/Data-Ink_Ratio) asks how much data each drop of ink conveys. Aim to maximize it, with good judgment.

- **7 ± 2:** The human mind can hold only [7 ± 2 concepts](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magical_Number_Seven,_Plus_or_Minus_Two) in attention at once. Cramming too many elements onto a slide increases mental effort and fatigue, making the presentation less effective.

- **Meaning:** The meaning of what you show—diagrams, charts, formulas—must be clear. Either it follows a convention your audience knows, or you need to explain it explicitly.

# SUGGESTIONS

- Be punctual and ready when your turn comes. If you are using your own computer, have it on with the presentation open. If you are using the room’s computer, load your presentation before the session begins.

- Each slide takes one or two minutes to present, sometimes longer. If you have 20 minutes to speak, prepare no more than 10–15 slides.

- Plan your timing carefully so you do not exceed your allotted time. Going over shows poor organization and a lack of respect for the committee and the students presenting after you. It is a disaster.

- Prepare what you will say and rehearse it a few times so you can make the best use of the time available.

- Look at your slides on a computer monitor from two or three meters away. If something is unreadable, remove it or make it larger. Otherwise, it is just a patch of color that conveys no information.

- Do not read your slides aloud. It gives the strong impression that you do not know the subject or, at best, that you did not prepare properly.

- Do not put long sentences or passages of text on your slides. If you read them aloud, you will be boring; if the audience reads them, they will not listen to you.

- Limit the number of items or elements on each slide. Otherwise, the audience may struggle to absorb them and pay too much attention to the slides and too little to what you say (7 ± 2).

- Take care with your charts, and above all avoid [pie charts](http://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/08-21-07.pdf) and [3D charts](http://www.perceptualedge.com/example3.php).

- Pay attention to image quality. Use [vector formats](http://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafica_vettoriale) where possible, and avoid [raster images](http://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafica_raster) that may appear pixelated.

- Avoid ending with a “Thank you” slide or a question mark. Use the final slide to recap your work and provide a backdrop for the discussion, perhaps [with thumbnails](http://andreas-zeller.blogspot.it/2013/10/summarizing-your-presentation-with.html).

- Avoid opening with an agenda or contents slide unless your presentation is long and you will refer back to it as you speak.

# REFERENCES

These are a few essential suggestions, but there is much more to learn about giving a good presentation and displaying data effectively.

A short reading list I strongly recommend:

- E. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 2nd ed., Graphics Press, 2001.
- G. Reynolds, *Presentation Zen: Simple Ideas on Presentation Design and Delivery*, 2nd ed., New Riders, 2011.
- S. Few, *Show Me the Numbers: Designing Tables and Graphs to Enlighten*, 2nd ed., Analytics Press, 2012.

For those who want to explore further, all three authors also maintain interesting websites or blogs:

- [The Work of Edward Tufte and Graphics Press](http://www.edwardtufte.com/tufte/)
- [Presentation Zen](http://www.presentationzen.com/)
- [Perceptual Edge](http://www.perceptualedge.com/)
